About this project

Gustavo Valença is a Brazilian-trained lawyer with experience leading legal teams, working across Python/Django development and data privacy law. This tool takes a body of legal knowledge and turns it into something a technical team can actually run, query, and extend, rather than writing another article about data protection law.

LGPD, GDPR, and CCPA/CPRA share a common ancestry and a common goal — giving individuals more control over their personal data — but they diverge in real, consequential ways: who they cover, what makes processing lawful, what a business must document, and who can sue whom. Those divergences are exactly what a privacy or data governance team has to track day to day, usually across spreadsheets, memos, and institutional memory. This project asks a narrower, more concrete question: what does that comparison look like when it's modeled as data instead of prose, and what can you build once it is?

Why this project, and why it's built this way

  • Legal content as structured data, not a static document. Every comparison point lives in a Django model (Category × Law → ComparisonEntry), editable through the admin rather than hardcoded into a template. Adding a new comparison dimension — say, international data transfer rules — means adding rows, not rewriting pages. It's the same shape a real compliance team's internal knowledge base would take.
  • A working tool, not just a reference. The "Generate compliance summary" feature takes a short factual questionnaire about a business and produces a downloadable PDF stating which frameworks are triggered and why — the same first move a privacy analyst makes when a business team asks "does this law apply to us?" The underlying applicability logic (an auditable set of AND/OR rules per law, not scattered if-statements) is unit-tested, because a compliance tool that quietly gives the wrong answer is worse than no tool at all.
  • Accuracy disciplined by process, not by memory. Every legal fact in this project's seed data — every article citation, every fine amount, every threshold — was checked against a primary or regulatory source (planalto.gov.br, eur-lex.europa.eu, leginfo.legislature.ca.gov, cppa.ca.gov) and is marked as verified, with the specific source and the date it was checked recorded alongside it. Figures that change on a statutory schedule (CCPA/CPRA's inflation-adjusted thresholds and fine amounts, for instance) are flagged with when they're next due to move. Nothing on this site is legal advice, but nothing on it is guesswork, either.

Why it matters

A data governance and privacy analyst spends much of their time doing exactly this kind of translation: reading statutory text, distilling it into rules an organization can actually operationalize, and keeping that mapping current as the law changes. This tool covers both halves: the legal analysis behind each rule, and the implementation that makes it queryable, testable, and auditable.

Tech stack

Django (data model, admin, views/templates), vanilla JavaScript for client-side filtering, and ReportLab for PDF generation, configured to deploy on Vercel with Postgres — deliberately conventional choices so every line of the implementation is easy to explain and defend, not just something that works. See the project README for the full technical write-up.

Related tools

This is one of four related tools. Each one targets a different piece of what a privacy/data governance analyst actually does, and together they follow a processing activity through its life cycle:

  1. LGPD-GDPR-CCPA-Comparative-Analysis (this project) — which laws apply, and how they differ.
  2. Data-Mapping-ROPA — recording the processing activities a company already runs, as a queryable Record of Processing Activities. Live demo · Code
  3. DPIA-Privacy-Impact-Assessment — assessing a new processing activity before it launches, with a guided workflow, a risk score, and an exportable report. Live demo · Code
  4. Incident-Breach-Response — what to do when something goes wrong: which breach notifications LGPD, GDPR and California law require, to whom, and by when, with a response checklist and an exportable incident report. Live demo · Code

The other three tools share one fictional e-commerce company, so the same scenario can be followed from the data map to the impact assessment to the incident response.
